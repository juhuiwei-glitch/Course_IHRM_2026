## High-Performance Work System

Organizational performance does not depend on a single factor. A company may have talented employees, advanced technology, strong managers, or attractive rewards, but these elements do not automatically produce high performance by themselves. What matters is how well the different parts of the organization work together.

This idea is central to the concept of a <span class="key-term">high-performance work system (HPWS)</span>. An HPWS is an organization in which the major elements of work are designed to support one another and help the organization achieve its goals. These elements include people, task design, organizational structure, reward systems, information systems, and technology.

The people in the organization need the skills and preparation required for their jobs. Task design determines how work is organized into jobs or team responsibilities. Organizational structure defines how people are grouped and how responsibilities and reporting relationships are arranged. Reward systems encourage employees to pursue particular goals and behaviors. Information systems allow important information to be shared throughout the organization, while technology supports the way work is performed, coordinated, and communicated.

The important idea is that these elements should not be considered separately. A high-performance work system depends on how well they fit together as a functioning whole.

<div class="section-number" id="alignment">02</div>

## Alignment: Do the HR Practices Send the Same Message?

One way to understand a high-performance work system is through the idea of <span class="key-term">alignment</span>. HR practices should support the same organizational priorities and reinforce one another rather than create conflicting incentives.

For example, if teamwork is important, an organization might recruit people who work well with others, provide opportunities to develop teamwork skills, evaluate employees partly on their contribution to the team, and reward successful team performance. In this case, the different HR practices reinforce the same message.

In contrast, an organization may recruit and train employees for teamwork but evaluate and reward only individual competition. Employees then receive conflicting signals about what behavior is actually valued.

This systems perspective is important because employees do not learn what the organization values from one policy alone. They experience the entire HR system. The combined effect of recruitment, job design, training, performance management, compensation, and other practices helps shape employee behavior.

<div class="section-number" id="how-hrm-contributes">03</div>

## How Does HRM Contribute to High Performance?

Human resource management contributes to high performance through several connected practices.

<div class="accent-block" markdown="1">

<span class="label">Job design</span>

Job design determines how work and responsibilities are organized. Effective job design can give employees opportunities to use their skills, cooperate with others, and contribute meaningfully to organizational goals.

<span class="label">Recruitment and selection</span>

Recruitment and selection help the organization attract and choose people whose abilities and characteristics fit the work that needs to be performed.

<span class="label">Training and development</span>

Training and development help employees acquire the knowledge and skills required for current work and prepare them for future responsibilities.

<span class="label">Performance management</span>

Performance management communicates expectations, measures employee performance, and provides feedback. It helps employees understand how their work contributes to organizational goals.

<span class="label">Compensation and rewards</span>

Compensation and rewards can reinforce the behaviors and results that the organization wants to encourage.

</div>

These practices are most effective when they support one another. Their value does not come only from having strong recruitment, training, performance management, or compensation systems separately, but from combining them in a way that supports the organization’s overall goals.

<div class="section-number" id="characteristics-of-an-hpws">04</div>

## Commonly Cited Characteristics of a High-Performance Work System

There is no single formula that every high-performance organization must follow. Different organizations may emphasize different practices depending on their strategy, industry, workforce, and work processes. However, several characteristics are commonly associated with high-performance work systems.

1. **Team-based work.** Work is often organized around teams rather than only around isolated individual jobs. Teams can support coordination, problem solving, and knowledge sharing.

2. **Employee participation in selection.** Employees may participate in decisions about selecting new coworkers, especially when teamwork and interpersonal fit are important.

3. **Performance feedback and improvement.** Employees receive formal performance feedback and are involved in improving their own performance rather than being treated only as passive recipients of evaluation.

4. **Ongoing learning and training.** Continuous development is emphasized, and employees are encouraged and rewarded for acquiring and applying new knowledge and skills.

5. **Performance-related rewards.** Compensation and rewards may be connected to the organization’s performance so that employees have incentives to contribute to broader organizational success.

6. **Flexible technology and work processes.** Equipment, technology, and work processes are designed to support flexibility, communication, and interaction among employees.

7. **Participation in workplace changes.** Employees may be involved in decisions about changes to equipment, work methods, layout, or other aspects of how work is performed.

8. **Broad use of skills.** Jobs are designed so that employees can use a variety of skills rather than performing only narrow and repetitive tasks.

9. **Understanding the larger work system.** Employees understand how their jobs contribute to the final product or service and how their work connects with the work of others.

10. **Ethical behavior.** High-performance organizations also support ethical standards. Ethical expectations can be reinforced through training, performance management, rewards, organizational policies, and the behavior of leaders.

These characteristics should be understood as examples of conditions that can support high performance rather than as a universal checklist. The broader principle remains the same: organizations perform better when their people, work systems, HR practices, and organizational goals are aligned and mutually supportive.

<div class="section-number" id="purposes-of-performance-management">05</div>

## Purposes of Performance Management

<span class="key-term">Performance management</span> serves several purposes within an organization. Three of the most important are strategic, administrative, and developmental.

<span class="label">Strategic purpose</span>

Performance management helps connect employee behavior and performance with organizational goals. Performance measures should therefore reflect what the organization is trying to achieve and make clear what employees are expected to contribute.

<span class="label">Administrative purpose</span>

Performance information supports decisions about pay, promotion, retention, reassignment, and termination. Because these decisions can have major consequences for employees, performance information should be as accurate and fair as possible.

<span class="label">Developmental purpose</span>

Performance management can identify employees’ strengths, weaknesses, and areas for improvement. Useful feedback helps employees understand what they are doing well and what they need to develop rather than simply giving them a score.

<div class="section-number" id="criteria-for-effective-performance-management">06</div>

## Criteria for Effective Performance Management

An effective performance management system should produce information that is useful, credible, and connected with organizational goals. Five important criteria are fit with strategy, validity, reliability, acceptability, and specific feedback.

<span class="label">Fit with strategy</span>

Performance measures should support the organization’s goals and priorities. Employees can contribute more effectively when the system evaluates behaviors and results that genuinely matter to the organization.

<span class="label">Validity</span>

Validity refers to whether a performance measure actually captures the performance dimension it is intended to measure. A valid measure should represent the important aspects of that dimension and avoid being influenced too much by irrelevant factors. For example, if a salesperson’s communication ability is evaluated mainly on how confidently they speak, the measure may overlook other important aspects of communication, such as listening to customers, understanding their needs, explaining information clearly, and responding appropriately. A measure can be clear and easy to use, but have low validity.

<span class="label">Reliability</span>

Reliability refers to consistency. When the same performance dimension is evaluated under similar conditions, the measure should produce reasonably consistent results. For example, if two managers evaluate the same employee’s teamwork during the same period, but one rates the employee very highly while the other gives a very low rating, the measure may have low reliability across raters. Reliability can also involve consistency over time: if the employee’s actual level of teamwork has not changed, the rating should not change dramatically simply because the evaluation occurs on a different occasion.

<span class="label">Acceptability</span>

A performance measure should be acceptable to the people who use it. Managers and employees are more likely to take a system seriously when it is seen as reasonable, understandable, and practical. Even a technically strong measure may fail if people consider it unfair or too complicated to use.

<span class="label">Specific feedback</span>

Performance measures should provide clear information about expectations and improvement. Comments such as “good attitude” or “needs improvement” are too vague to guide behavior. Specific feedback helps employees understand what they should continue doing and what they should change.

<div class="section-number" id="methods-for-measuring-performance">07</div>

## Methods for Measuring Performance

Once an organization decides what good performance means, it needs a method for measuring it. Some methods compare employees with one another, while others focus on individual attributes, behaviors, or results.

### Making Comparisons

Comparison methods evaluate an employee relative to other employees rather than against an independent performance standard.

<span class="key-term">Simple ranking</span> requires managers to rank employees from the highest performer to the lowest performer. It is easy to understand, but it shows only relative order and does not indicate the absolute level of performance.

<span class="key-term">Forced distribution</span> places predetermined percentages of employees into performance categories, such as 20% high performers, 60% average performers, and 20% low performers. This can prevent managers from rating everyone highly, but the required distribution may not reflect the actual distribution of employee performance.

<span class="key-term">Paired comparison</span> compares each employee with every other employee. The employee who “wins” the most comparisons receives the highest ranking. This approach can be systematic, but it becomes increasingly complicated as the number of employees grows.

### Rating Attributes

Attribute approaches evaluate characteristics that are believed to be important for successful job performance. A common method is the <span class="key-term">graphic rating scale</span>, in which employees are rated on several dimensions such as job knowledge, communication, judgment, quality of work, teamwork, initiative, creativity, or problem solving.

Attribute ratings are relatively easy to create and use. However, broad characteristics such as initiative or communication may be interpreted differently by different raters, and an attribute score may provide limited guidance about what an employee should actually do differently.

### Rating Behaviors

Behavior-based approaches focus on what employees actually do on the job.

The <span class="key-term">critical-incident method</span> requires managers to record specific examples of particularly effective or ineffective employee behavior. These examples can later provide concrete evidence for feedback.

A <span class="key-term">behaviorally anchored rating scale (BARS)</span> defines different performance levels using specific behavioral descriptions. For example, for interpersonal relationships, a high level might be described as “is on good terms with everyone and works well with others even when they disagree,” a medium level as “gets along with most people and has only occasional, usually minor, conflicts,” and a low level as “tends to become involved in unnecessary conflicts with others.” Rather than giving only a number, BARS explains what different performance levels actually look like.

A <span class="key-term">behavioral observation scale (BOS)</span> also uses defined behaviors, but instead of asking what level of performance a behavior represents, it asks how frequently the employee demonstrates the behavior—for example, never, sometimes, regularly, or almost always.

<span class="key-term">Organizational behavior modification (OBM)</span> connects performance measurement with feedback and reinforcement. The organization identifies important behaviors, measures whether employees demonstrate them, communicates expectations, and then uses feedback and reinforcement to encourage desired behavior.

### Measuring Results

Results-based approaches evaluate what employees or teams achieve. Measures may include productivity, sales, revenue, customer satisfaction, quality, project completion, or other outcomes.

<span class="key-term">Management by Objectives (MBO)</span> is one important results-based approach. Goals are established throughout the organization so that individual and team objectives contribute to broader organizational goals. These objectives then become standards for evaluating performance.

MBO generally emphasizes three features: goals should be specific, challenging, and objective; managers and employees should participate together in setting goals; and managers should provide feedback during the performance period.

Results-based measures can be useful because outcomes are often easy to understand and communicate. However, employees do not always have complete control over results, and an excessive focus on measurable outcomes may cause important behaviors or long-term goals to be overlooked.

<div class="section-number" id="total-quality-management">08</div>

## Total Quality Management (TQM)

<span class="key-term">Total Quality Management (TQM)</span> is a management approach that emphasizes continuous improvement, customer satisfaction, and the involvement of employees throughout the organization. Rather than focusing only on whether individual employees meet performance targets, TQM encourages organizations to examine the quality of work processes, identify sources of errors or inefficiency, and continuously improve how work is performed.

Performance information may therefore include measures such as product or service quality, customer complaints, error rates, process efficiency, and customer satisfaction. TQM is not simply another performance appraisal method; instead, it provides a broader philosophy in which performance measurement is used to support continuous improvement across the organization.

<div class="section-number" id="sources-of-performance-information">09</div>

## Sources of Performance Information

Performance information can come from several sources, including <span class="key-term">managers</span>, <span class="key-term">peers</span>, <span class="key-term">subordinates</span>, <span class="key-term">employees themselves</span>, and <span class="key-term">customers</span>. Different sources may observe different aspects of an employee’s performance, so organizations sometimes combine information from multiple sources.

A <span class="key-term">360-degree performance appraisal</span> collects performance information from several of these sources rather than relying only on the employee’s direct manager. For example, an employee may receive feedback from their supervisor, coworkers, subordinates, customers, and themselves. The purpose is to provide a more complete view of performance by incorporating different perspectives.

<div class="section-number" id="errors-in-performance-measurement">10</div>

## Errors in Performance Measurement

Performance ratings are not always completely objective. Managers may make systematic judgment errors that affect how employees are evaluated.

<span class="label">Similarity error</span>

Similarity error occurs when a manager gives higher ratings to employees who are similar to them in characteristics, attitudes, background, or work style. The evaluation may therefore reflect how much the manager identifies with the employee rather than the employee’s actual performance.

<span class="label">Contrast error</span>

Contrast error occurs when an employee is evaluated in comparison with other employees rather than against an appropriate performance standard. For example, an average employee may appear very strong when evaluated immediately after a poor performer, or appear weak when compared with an exceptionally strong performer.

<span class="label">Distributional errors</span>

Distributional errors occur when a manager tends to use only part of the rating scale. A <span class="key-term">leniency error</span> occurs when ratings are consistently too high, while a <span class="key-term">strictness error</span> occurs when ratings are consistently too low. <span class="key-term">Central tendency</span> occurs when a manager avoids very high or very low ratings and places most employees near the middle of the scale.

<span class="label">Halo error</span>

Halo error occurs when one positive characteristic or impression influences the manager’s ratings of other aspects of performance. For example, an employee who communicates very confidently may also receive high ratings for teamwork or judgment even when there is limited evidence for those ratings.

<span class="label">Horns error</span>

Horns error is the opposite of halo error. One negative characteristic or event creates an overall negative impression and lowers the employee’s ratings across several performance dimensions.

<span class="label">Recency error</span>

Recency error occurs when a manager gives too much weight to an employee’s most recent performance and does not adequately consider performance across the entire evaluation period. A recent success or failure may therefore have an unusually strong influence on the final rating.

<div class="section-number" id="legal-and-ethical-issues">11</div>

## Legal and Ethical Issues in Performance Management

Performance management does not only involve questions of accuracy and effectiveness. It also raises important legal and ethical issues, particularly when performance judgments may be influenced by bias or when organizations collect detailed information about employees through technology.

### Discrimination

Many performance measures involve subjective judgment, so bias can easily influence ratings. Research has shown that raters may sometimes give more favorable evaluations to people who are similar to themselves, including members of their own racial or social group. Stereotypes can also influence how particular traits are interpreted. For example, the same behavior may be viewed differently depending on the employee’s gender, and qualities such as leadership, assertiveness, or communication may therefore be rated inconsistently.

These biases can affect not only performance scores but also important decisions involving promotion, pay, development opportunities, or disciplinary action. Performance evaluations should therefore be based as much as possible on clear, job-related criteria and observable evidence rather than on general impressions or personal characteristics unrelated to the job. Organizations should also apply performance standards consistently across employees.

### Electronic Monitoring and Employee Privacy

Technology has made it increasingly easy for organizations to collect detailed information about employee behavior and performance. Electronic monitoring may include computer activity, communication records, location data, time spent on tasks, productivity measures, or other forms of digital information.

Such information can help organizations understand work patterns and identify performance problems. However, monitoring can also raise important ethical concerns about privacy, autonomy, and trust. Employees may feel uncomfortable when they are monitored extensively, especially if they do not know what information is being collected or how it will be used.

For this reason, organizations should consider not only whether monitoring is technically possible, but also whether it is necessary, proportionate, and transparent. Employees should have a clear understanding of what information is collected, why it is collected, and how it may influence performance-related decisions.

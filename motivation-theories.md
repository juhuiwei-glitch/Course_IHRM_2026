Why do some employees stay highly engaged while others gradually lose motivation? Why does the same reward motivate one person but have little effect on another? And why do salary, recognition, autonomy, responsibility, or promotion sometimes work—but sometimes not?

Motivation theories help us understand these questions. Rather than assuming that there is one best way to motivate everyone, different theories highlight different parts of the motivational process. Some focus on what people need from work, some explain how people evaluate effort and rewards, and others show how managers can direct effort toward performance.

This page introduces five influential theories of work motivation from three complementary perspectives:

- **What people need from work**
- **How people evaluate effort and outcomes**
- **How managers can direct motivated effort**

<div class="section-number">Part 1</div>

## What Do People Need from Work?

Two important theories in this category are **Self-Determination Theory** and **Herzberg’s Two-Factor Theory**. Both suggest that motivation is not simply a matter of giving employees more money or rewards. The experience of the work itself also matters.

### Self-Determination Theory

Self-Determination Theory proposes that people are more likely to experience high-quality motivation when <span class="key-term">three basic psychological needs</span> are supported:

<div class="accent-block" markdown="1">

<span class="label">Autonomy</span>

People want to feel that they have some choice and control over how they act. This does not mean that employees can do anything they want. Rather, they are more likely to be motivated when they understand the purpose of their work and have meaningful room to make decisions.

<span class="label">Competence</span>

People want to feel capable and to experience growth and mastery. Work can become motivating when it provides an appropriate level of challenge, opportunities to develop skills, and feedback that helps people improve.

<span class="label">Relatedness</span>

People want to feel connected to others and to feel that they belong. Supportive relationships with coworkers, supervisors, and other members of the organization can therefore influence motivation.

</div>

<div class="case-connection post-discussion" markdown="1">

<div class="case-connection-title">Connection to the Case</div>

<span class="case-character">Charlie Chao — Autonomy</span>

Charlie is highly capable but seems less enthusiastic about carrying out assignments from his supervisors. Giving him greater freedom to make decisions and take action (#11) may increase his sense of autonomy.

<span class="case-character">Grace Ge — Competence</span>

Grace is sharp-minded, highly driven, and well educated. Job enrichment (#8) could give her more challenging responsibilities and opportunities to use and further develop her abilities.

<span class="case-character">Lily Lo — Relatedness</span>

Lily is young, her work is monotonous, and some coworkers from her hometown have already left the factory. Helping her build stronger connections with coworkers and a greater sense of belonging (#16) may address an important source of motivation.

</div>

### Herzberg’s Two-Factor Theory

Herzberg’s Two-Factor Theory makes an important distinction between two groups of job-related factors: **hygiene factors** and **motivators**.

<span class="label">Hygiene factors</span>

Hygiene factors include aspects such as salary, company policies, supervision, working conditions, job security, and relationships at work. When these factors are poor, employees may become dissatisfied. Improving them can reduce dissatisfaction. However, according to Herzberg, simply improving hygiene factors does not necessarily create strong motivation.

<span class="label">Motivators</span>

Motivators are factors related more directly to the content of the work itself, such as achievement, recognition, responsibility, advancement, growth, and meaningful or challenging work. These factors are more likely to create satisfaction and stronger motivation.

**A key idea in Herzberg’s theory is that reducing dissatisfaction is not necessarily the same as creating motivation.**

<div class="case-connection post-discussion" markdown="1">

<div class="case-connection-title">Connection to the Case</div>

<span class="case-character">Olivia Ou</span>

Because Olivia is married with two children, it may be tempting to assume that a salary increase or bonus would be the best way to motivate her. However, she is already a highly capable head nurse and is pursuing further education while working.

Herzberg’s theory reminds us that improving financial rewards may reduce dissatisfaction or make the job more attractive, but may not necessarily create stronger motivation. Giving Olivia greater participation in management decisions (#10), for example, may provide more responsibility, involvement, and opportunity for growth.

</div>

## How Do People Decide Whether Effort Is Worthwhile?

Motivation also depends on how employees evaluate the relationship between their effort, performance, and outcomes. Two theories are especially useful here: **Expectancy Theory** and **Equity Theory**.

### Expectancy Theory

Expectancy Theory proposes that motivation is strongest when <span class="key-term">all three</span> of the following beliefs are present:

<div class="accent-block" markdown="1">

<span class="label">Expectancy</span>

**“If I put in more effort, can I perform well?”** Employees are more likely to invest effort when they believe that their effort can actually improve their performance. Skills, resources, training, confidence, and working conditions can all influence expectancy.

<span class="label">Instrumentality</span>

**“If I perform well, will it lead to an outcome?”** Employees need to believe that strong performance will actually lead to a meaningful consequence, such as a bonus, recognition, promotion, greater responsibility, or another valued outcome.

<span class="label">Valence</span>

**“Do I actually value that outcome?”** Even if a reward is available, it will not be motivating if the employee does not care much about it.

</div>

<div class="formula">Motivation = Expectancy × Instrumentality × Valence</div>

<div class="case-connection post-discussion" markdown="1">

<div class="case-connection-title">Connection to the Case</div>

<span class="case-character">Howard Hung — Valence</span>

Howard already receives investment income that is about twice his annual salary. A small salary increase may therefore have relatively low value for him. Further education or professional development (#14), for example, may be more attractive.

<span class="case-character">Leo Lee — Expectancy</span>

Leo has strong technical ability but does not appear to have strong communication or administrative skills. Promoting him directly into a managerial role may not only be risky for the organization, but may also be less motivating if Leo himself does not feel confident that he can perform the role successfully. A technical expert career path may better match his strengths and give him a more realistic opportunity to succeed.

</div>

### Equity Theory

Equity Theory focuses on employees’ perceptions of fairness. People do not evaluate their work situation only by looking at what they receive in absolute terms; they also compare their own contributions and outcomes with those of others. For example, employees may consider how much effort, experience, skill, education, time, or responsibility they contribute, and compare these inputs with outcomes such as salary, recognition, promotion, status, or opportunities. When employees feel that the balance between what they contribute and what they receive is unfair compared with others, their motivation may decrease.

<div class="case-connection post-discussion" markdown="1">

<div class="case-connection-title">Connection to the Case</div>

<span class="case-character">Leo Lee</span>

Leo has made major contributions to research, but several chemists with similar experience have moved into managerial positions while he has not. His morale has declined, which may partly reflect a perceived imbalance between his contributions and the career outcomes he receives compared with his peers.

However, simply promoting him into a managerial role may not be the best solution because he does not appear to have strong administrative or communication skills. A different career path—such as a senior technical expert position (#13)—could provide recognition and advancement while better matching his strengths.

</div>

## How Can Managers Direct Motivated Effort?

Even when employees are motivated, their effort still needs direction. **Goal-Setting Theory** explains how goals can help translate motivation into performance.

### Goal-Setting Theory

Goal-Setting Theory proposes that goals are more effective when they are specific, challenging, and achievable. A clear goal helps employees understand what they are trying to accomplish, while an appropriately challenging goal can encourage greater effort and persistence.

The level of challenge also matters. If a goal is too easy, it may not encourage much effort; if it is perceived as impossible, employees may lose confidence or commitment. Effective goals therefore need to provide meaningful challenge while remaining realistic for the person’s current abilities and available resources.

Feedback is also important because employees need to know how they are progressing and whether they should adjust their effort or strategy. **In this sense, choosing a motivational method is only one step; managers also need to help employees understand what they are working toward.**
